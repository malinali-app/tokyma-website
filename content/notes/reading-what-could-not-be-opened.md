Some translation problems are not missing parallel text. They are objects that cannot be opened. [Amy Briggs, writing for National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/premium/article/decoding-ancient-scrolls-ai-technology), traces a line from Champollion at the Rosetta Stone and George Smith among cuneiform tablets — decades of comparison, by hand — to tools that recover writing from things once thought lost.

The Ein Gedi scroll, burned to a charcoal tube in a synagogue fire on the Dead Sea’s western shore, was too fragile to unroll. In 2015 a team combined CT scans with software that built a virtual copy and unfurled *that*. The inner layers held the first two chapters of Leviticus; the object itself stayed intact. A similar logic, with DNA this time, has been applied to fragments of the Dead Sea Scrolls: animal-skin parchment from different animals gives conservators another signal when thousands of pieces have to be reassembled.

The Herculaneum library is the louder recent case. Vesuvius carbonized some 1,800 scrolls in 79 A.D. They have been known since 1752. Early physical unrolling destroyed many of them. The [Vesuvius Challenge](https://scrollprize.org/), launched in 2023, released high-resolution scans and asked machine-learning teams to read ink that the eye cannot see. Within a year a team had recovered a few percent of one scroll — described, not without humour, as a two-thousand-year-old note on how to enjoy life. The contest has since moved toward whole volumes; in 2026 one scroll, PHerc. 1667, was reported as read end to end. That is not “AI decoded Latin.” It is imaging, geometry, and models trained to find carbon ink in a tomography volume, with philologists still doing the reading.

The Voynich manuscript sits at the other end of the same fascination. The vellum is fifteenth century, the script is looping and still unread, the pictures suggest botany, astronomy, medicine. Computer scientists have tried. Nothing has held up. Yale has [put the book online](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/premium/article/decoding-ancient-scrolls-ai-technology). It is waiting, as the Herculaneum lumps waited.

The lesson for living languages is modest. New tools can unlock sources that were closed for technical reasons — heat, fragmentation, ink that does not contrast. They do not replace the slow work of knowing what a sign means, or to whom. Champollion had Greek. The Vesuvius teams have a known language once the letters appear. A language without a Rosetta Stone is a different problem, even when the imaging is perfect.
