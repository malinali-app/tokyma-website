In 2008 the biologist Shane Gero listened to two young sperm whales click back and forth for forty minutes off Dominica. He had years of recordings. He still could not say what they were doing with the sounds. The insight he kept, [Craig Welch later wrote for National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/scientists-plan-to-use-ai-to-try-to-decode-the-language-of-whales), was that decoding would need the clicks *and* the scene: who was speaking, which family, what the animals were doing.

[Project CETI](https://www.projectceti.org/) (Cetacean Translation Initiative) was built on that bet. Linguists, roboticists, and machine-learning researchers joined marine biologists. The wager was not that whales would learn English. It was that sequence models — the same family of methods that now move between human languages without an aligned dictionary — might find structure in a communication system that has no Rosetta Stone. Gero already had annotated recordings. The project set out to gather orders of magnitude more, with hydrophones in the water column and cameras that can follow a whale into the dark.

By 2024, CETI and MIT CSAIL had proposed a [“sperm whale phonetic alphabet”](https://news.mit.edu/2024/csail-ceti-explores-sperm-whale-alphabet-0507): rhythm, tempo, rubato, and ornamentation combining into a large inventory of distinguishable codas. Later work with UC Berkeley described discrete spectral patterns that the authors analogise, carefully, to vowels. None of this is a dictionary. Linguists still argue over whether non-human communication should be called language at all. The papers claim combinatorial structure that had not been visible when the clicks were treated as Morse.

The honest parallel with low-resource human languages is methodological, not mystical. Both problems starve without context. A whale coda without behaviour is a news sentence without a speaker. A translation model without register is a plausible string. Machine learning can find units in a signal that human annotators were too few to chart. It cannot decide, by itself, what those units are *for*. Gero’s remark still sits under the project: the question “what will you say to them?” assumes they have a language for talking about us.

We study an industry that is changing quickly. Some of that change is happening far from localisation — in tomography of burned papyrus, in hydrophones off Dominica. The tools overlap. The caution does too. New methods open problems that were locked for years. They do not abolish the need to know who is speaking, and to whom.
